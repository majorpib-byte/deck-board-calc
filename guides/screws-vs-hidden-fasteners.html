Fasteners

Deck screws and hidden fasteners

The screw line on the board calculator is two fasteners every time a board crosses a joist. A clip system does not use that line. Count the product you are actually installing.

Last updated October 25, 2026

On the opening deck, 16 feet along the house and 12 feet out, boards run along the house. Twenty-six rows cover the surface. Joists at 16 inches on center, both ends included, are 13. Two screws at each crossing is 26 × 13 × 2 = 676 screws. Add the same 10 percent waste used on the boards and you buy 744. That arithmetic is on the board calculator. It is a shopping estimate for face screws. It is not a statement that two screws satisfy your board maker, and it is not a clip count.

Where the two screws go

Each deck board should be fastened at every joist it crosses, near both edges, so the board cannot cup up on one side. The usual face-screw habit is one screw toward each edge, set in from the end so you do not split the board. Ends that land on a joist still get two. A butt joint in the middle of a spliced row needs fastening on both sides of the joint, which means the joist under that joint is doing two jobs: holding the ends and giving the screws wood. The cut list does not draw that joist. You still have to land the joint on one.

Edges of the deck, the rim, and the ledger side are still crossings. Do not skip the first and last joist because they are “the frame.” They are in the 13 for a reason. Skip them and the screw total looks thrifty while the outside board lifts.

Match the screw to the board

Pressure-treated lumber eats plain steel. Use a screw made for treated wood: a coating the maker rates for that use, or stainless where the deck stays wet or the board is cedar and you do not want streaks. Composite and PVC often require the screw that brand names, sometimes a particular color and a particular head that sets flush with a depth guide. A construction screw from a bulk bin can mushroom the surface or void the board warranty. Read the sheet that came with the decking before you buy 744 of the wrong head.

Length has to reach through the decking and well into the joist. A 5/4 board is about an inch thick, so a short screw is mostly thread in the decking and a bite of the joist. The decking maker’s fastener chart is the one to follow. This page will not assign a universal inch length, because a 2×6 deck board is thicker than 5/4 and a clip system may not use a face screw at all.

Drive them straight. A cammed-out head is a future leak into the joist and a board you cannot remove cleanly. Set a clutch or a depth nose so the head is flush, not buried in a crater. The 10 percent waste covers bent screws and a few you will back out. It does not cover a whole box of the wrong coating.

Hidden clips are a different shopping line

A grooved-edge board usually takes one clip at each joist, between this board and the next, plus a starter strip or starter clips along the first row against the house, and often a face screw or a finishing clip on the last row where a groove no longer has a neighbor. On the opening deck the installed rows are 26 and the joists are 13, so the between-board crossings are on the order of 26 × 13 if you clip every row including a starter pattern, or one fewer interval if the starter replaces the first clip line. Read the picture on the box. The safe habit is to count what that picture shows per joist, times 13 joists, times your rows, then add the starter package it tells you to add.

Do not type 744 into the clip order. Those were screws. Clips are fewer pieces and usually more dollars each. The cost guide shows an example: 338 clips at a made-up $1.25 is a few hundred dollars, replacing the screw line rather than adding to it. Use your box price. Butts in a spliced row often need a special clip or a sistered block. A 20 foot row of two 10s has one butt per row. Multiply those before you leave the store.

Clips also set the side gap. If you gap with a nail and also use a clip, you are fighting the system. Install at the temperature the sheet assumes, because plastic moves and the clip will not grow to hide a mistake.

Diagonal boards and picture frames

Diagonal decking crosses joists on a longer path and often at a spacing the manufacturer limits to 12 inches on center. The crossing count goes up, and the end cuts multiply waste. The rectangle calculator assumes boards perpendicular to the joists. Do not use 744 screws as the diagonal number. Recount crossings on a sketch, or tighten the joist spacing and run the calculator again so the joist count matches the frame you will build. A picture-frame border is a board around the edge, mitred or butted, and it is not one of the field rows. Add those linear feet by hand.

Repair is part of the choice

A face-screwed board can be backed out and replaced if you did not bury the heads. A clipped board in the middle of a grooved field sometimes means loosening a run back to the edge. That is a fair trade for a cleaner surface, and it is a reason to keep a few matching boards from the original lot. The waste percent on day one is also your repair stock. Three extra 16 foot boards on the opening deck are not laziness. They are the board you will want when a knot fails or a delivery scar shows up after the first rain.

Neither system makes the deck code-compliant. Fastener schedule, guard, and ledger are still local. The joist guide repeats the check: spacing for the screw count is the spacing you chose, and someone still has to confirm the joist can span. Buy the fasteners that match the board. Then stop and confirm the frame before those fasteners are the only thing holding a mistake in place.
