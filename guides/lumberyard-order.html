Plan the lumberyard order and the cut list

The calculator’s shopping list is the start of the conversation at the counter. It is not the whole cart, and it is not a cutting diagram with every joint marked.

Last updated October 25, 2026

Print the list before you drive. The board calculator has a Print list button that keeps the deck size, the cut plan, the line items, and the total, and drops the form and the long essay. A phone photo of the screen works if the printer is fussy. Either way, you want the lengths in writing so a helpful suggestion at the counter does not quietly change 16 foot boards into 12s.

Say the deck in one sentence

Lead with direction. “Sixteen feet along the house, twelve feet out, boards along the house, five-and-a-half-inch face, eighth-inch gap.” That sentence is the opening deck. It buys 29 boards at 16 feet after 10 percent waste. If you only say “twelve by sixteen,” the yard can price the other direction: 39 boards at 12 feet. Both are real. They are not interchangeable. The size guide puts the common rectangles side by side so you can point at the one you framed.

Then read the line items, not the square footage. Square footage is 192 on that deck. The order is 29 pieces of 16 foot decking, plus joists if you left them on: 13 joists that span 12 feet, one 16 foot rim, and screws. Prices on the page are placeholders. Cross them out and write the yard’s price before you agree to the total.

What the cut plan is telling you

On the opening deck every 16 foot board is one full row. There is no butt joint and no useful offcut. That is the easy cut list: 29 boards, each used whole, three of them held back as waste. Do not cut the waste boards “just to be efficient” before a row is fastened. Waste is the board you ruin, the board that is crowned too hard, and the board you cut an inch short.

A 14 foot run on 16 foot stock is different. Boards along the 14 foot side of a 14×20 need 16 foot boards trimmed by about 2 feet. The purchased length is longer than the deck. Those 2 foot offcuts are not a second row. The calculator refuses to pretend they are. Stack them for blocking or bracing if they are sound, or leave them. Do not reduce the order because you hope to rip scraps into decking.

A 20 foot run with 8, 10, 12, and 16 available becomes two 10s per row. The cut plan should say splice. Each splice needs a joist under the butt, and a small gap at the butt, which the gap guide separates from the side gap. Mark the joist before you cut a whole bundle to 9 feet 11 inches and discover the joint misses the framing.

Look at the boards before they are on the truck

Pressure-treated decking is not identical. Sight down each piece. A small crown can go up and work. A twist, a split end, or a knot cluster that breaks the edge should stay at the yard. The 10 percent waste is how you reject those without a second trip, but only if you reject them in the aisle. Once they are on your deck as fastened rows, the waste percent cannot help you.

Check moisture if you plan to stain soon. A board that is dark and heavy may be too wet for coating. That does not change today’s count. It changes whether the stain gallons get used this weekend or next season. Ask the yard how they store 16 foot stock. A bundle left in the sun with one face covered will have a curl you can see if you pull a board free.

Confirm the lengths they actually have. Uncheck a missing length in the calculator before you reprint. If 16s are gone and you switch a 16 foot run to 8s, every row becomes two pieces and the piece count jumps. Do that math in the parking lot, not after the truck is loaded with the wrong SKU.

Hardware the board list does not include

A deck is not only decking. The calculator’s joist line is the joists and one rim. It is not joist hangers, not structural screws or nails for those hangers, not a ledger, not ledger flashing, not bolts into the house, not post bases, not beam stock, and not the concrete under the posts. The footing calculator counts bags for a round tube after you already know the diameter and the depth. It will not choose a frost depth for you. Joist spacing explains the count. It is not a span table. Check local code before the order includes beams.

Write those extras under the printed list in your own handwriting: hanger model, how many, post-base model, flashing, and the ledger fasteners the detail calls for. If you do not have a detail, you are not ready for the ledger bolts. Buy the decking if you must lock a price. Do not invent a bolt schedule from a shopping site.

Delivery, and the cut list at home

Sixteen-foot boards need a vehicle that can carry them without a huge overhang, or a delivery. An overhang that whips on the highway cracks ends. Tell the yard the truth about your rack. It is cheaper than a second bundle.

At home, stage by length. Sixteens in one pile, twelves in another, joists crowned and marked. Cut from the plan, not from memory of the plan. The print footer repeats that the page is an estimate, not an IRC document. That line is there so the paper in your pocket does not get treated as a permit drawing when a helper asks “is this to code?” The answer is still no. The answer to “did we buy the rows we counted?” should be yes, because you can point at 29 boards and 26 rows and know which three are spare.

If the built deck is an inch off the plan, rerun the calculator before the second trip. An inch on a 16 foot side can add a joist, as the spacing guide shows. An inch on the across side can add a row when you were sitting just under a round-up. The list is cheap to reprint. The wrong row, fastened, is not.
